### Students@SC: Careers in HPC and AI
#### Jack Morrison, Senior AI/ML HPC Cluster Engineer, NVIDIA
Finding Your Place in AI/HPC
- Look well beyond job titles out there 
- You don't know what you'll actually be doing, so be open and consider what you really enjoy doing
- Engage/be active at conferences rather than a passive attendee. 
- Ask questions, interact with unknown folks at lunch for example. 
#### Panel and Q&A
**Subil Abraham**, ORNL, moderator

**Marc Henry de Frahan**, NVIDIA, manager for DevTech, code optimization for performance on GPUs

Q. How did you get here?
- **Lena**: Life Sciences undergrad who didn't think HPC and life sciences were different fields, being in domain science recognized the need for a bridge, found a position that ORNL 
- **Maria**: Computer Science undergrad in university of Athens, graduate studies in Canada in computer science, got involved with local startups in Canada, perf/databases/data science, came to the states, with ORNL for 4 years. Graduate studies really expanded my horizon and made my path more clear
- **Marc**: Masters thesis working with CUDA and GPUs for the first time, National Renewable Energy laboratory in Colorado for a lot of years, and then moved to NVIDIA
- **Spencer**: not a computer scientist or computational scientist. 

Q. What is one thing you wouldn't do in hindsight
- **Maria**: wouldn't change anything -- you learn a lot when things don't go as expected, and that experience helps you figure out what you want to do. Fail/Make mistakes and learn from them.
	- Side note: definitely work before you pursue grad school, industry experience between your bachelors and grad school really helps navigate grad school and beyond
- **Lena**: attitude shift -- hesitant to talk to people early before I was "ready"/had enough skills/was qualified. Someone said you are just a student, failing publicly is easiest as students. Wished 

Q. AI and AI tools being used extensively today. How do you find your work changing, or your collaboration with your colleagues?
- Marc: has shifted the focus to the really hard problems -- debugging LD_LIBRARY_PATH bugs or composing  dockerfiles were part of my job description, but the AI tools deal with that today and allow me to do the more interesting work.
- Maria: Tools (to codex or to claude is now a verb just like googling) - we are learning how to use these to ensure what we are doing is correct. We cannot avoid using these tools, 
- Spencer: mostly a view of deciding not just what can be solved, but what should be solved or partially solved using AI
- Lena: lower-level, common-question user tickets can be dealt with AI now. I have more ability to give users thorough answers, help resolve more tickets per day. 

Q. Skills you use day-to-day that you didn't learn in school
- Spencer: I don't remember
- Lena: Coding itself, since she did life sciences. Theory behind how you approach code. 
- Maria: basic concepts in my undergrad helped me move from C/C++ during undergrad to python after. More importantly, soft skills like conveying your ideas and listening to other people's ideas. 
- Marc: the "human stuff" - everything other than the technical stuff - how to communicate, distill your ideas and pass them on, getting feedback, incorporating feedback, how to be kind.

Q. There is a perception that moving between industry, national labs and academia may not be as easy, especially in certain directions like industry to academia for example. To those on the panel like Marc who did make a lateral move from national labs to industry, what made you decide to make that move and did you find anything challenging about that process? 
- Work sits at the intersection of computing and science, and national lab work was more science than math, and he wanted to do more math
- Interviews are always hard but there wasn't anything particularly challenging making that move

Q. Most common error that early career folks do
- Soft skills - nobody likes group projects, but they help with this sort of thing where you are working with people who have very different perspectives than you, sometimes for years. 
- self-awareness, being nervous about asking people questions - "wasting people's time as much as possible" - its up to you to stop people in the hallway and ask them questions and learn
- don't assume people know what you are talking about -- don't get into the weeds immediately, take a beat to set the context, why is it important, don't lose them in jargon
- **communication is important**
